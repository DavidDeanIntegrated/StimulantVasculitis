Stimulant Vasculitis / Vasculopathy

An interactive clinical education reference for emergency medicine and toxicology — from mechanism to management.

What Is Stimulant Vasculitis / Vasculopathy?

"Stimulant vasculitis" is a broad clinical term encompassing several overlapping mechanisms of vascular injury caused by stimulant drugs. It is not a single disease — rather, it is a spectrum of conditions that present along a continuum from pure vasospasm to frank inflammatory vasculitis. Understanding the range of injury patterns is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management in the emergency department.

Three Core Injury Patterns

1. Immune-Mediated Small-Vessel Vasculitis

Classic leukocytoclastic vasculitis, most strongly associated with levamisole-adulterated cocaine. Characterized by painful purpura, retiform skin lesions, and positive ANCA serology with neutropenia. Inflammatory infiltrates damage small vessel walls.

2. Thrombotic Vasculopathy / Ischemic Necrosis

Microvascular thrombosis causing tissue ischemia and skin necrosis. Often coexists with the inflammatory picture and may predominate on biopsy. Results from platelet activation and endothelial dysfunction rather than immune-mediated vessel wall destruction.

3. Catecholamine-Mediated Direct Vascular Injury

Sympathomimetic surge from cocaine and methamphetamine causes vasospasm, severe hypertension, endothelial injury, and platelet activation. This can produce ischemic injury without classic inflammatory infiltrates on biopsy.

Key Clinical Problem

Patients typically present with painful purpura, retiform (net-like) skin lesions, and tissue necrosis — with or without systemic organ involvement affecting the kidneys, lungs, or nervous system. The challenge lies in distinguishing between the different injury patterns and, critically, excluding dangerous mimics such as necrotizing soft tissue infection or endocarditis.

⚠️ High-Yield Association: Cocaine + Levamisole Adulteration → ANCA-positive vasculitis/vasculopathy + neutropenia. This is the most well-characterized stimulant vasculitis syndrome and should be the first pattern recognized in the ED. Levamisole has been found in over 70% of North American street cocaine at various points.

Why It Matters — ED Perspective

Common ED Presentation

Stimulant vasculitis frequently arrives as painful purpura, retiform (net-like) skin lesions, or frank skin necrosis, often with a note from an outside facility that simply says "vasculitis." The emergency physician must rapidly distinguish between drug-related causes and the dangerous mimics that require immediate intervention. A systematic approach to the differential diagnosis is critical for patient safety.

🚨 High-Risk Misses

The following conditions must be actively excluded before attributing findings to stimulant vasculopathy:

  • Infection and septic emboli — endocarditis, especially in IV drug users with fever and a new murmur
  • Purpura fulminans and DIC — rapidly progressive purpura with hemodynamic instability and coagulopathy
  • Necrotizing soft tissue infection (NSTI) — the most dangerous miss; pain out of proportion, crepitus, systemic toxicity
  • TTP / HUS — thrombocytopenia + renal failure + neurologic symptoms + microangiopathic hemolysis
  • Calciphylaxis — in patients with renal disease, painful skin calcifications with livedo pattern
  • Meningococcemia — rapidly progressive petechial/purpuric rash with sepsis

Who Is Affected

  • Most cases occur in adult cocaine and methamphetamine users, typically ages 20–50
  • Levamisole contamination of the cocaine supply has been estimated at over 70% in North American street cocaine at various points, making this an underrecognized but common exposure
  • Prescription stimulant vasculopathy (ADHD medications such as methylphenidate and dextroamphetamine) is less common but has been reported in the pediatric and young adult population

Why Levamisole Matters

Levamisole is an antihelminthic agent used in veterinary medicine that is added to cocaine as a cutting agent, likely because it enhances cocaine's euphoric effects through modulation of monoamine pathways. It causes immune dysregulation that persists well beyond cocaine's short half-life. Its immunologic effects — including ANCA positivity and agranulocytosis — can last for weeks with continued re-exposure. Levamisole is not detected on standard urine drug screens, making clinical suspicion the primary diagnostic tool.

Mechanisms of Toxicity

Cocaine

  • Cocaine has a short half-life (minutes to hours), but its effects can be prolonged through active metabolites such as cocaethylene (when combined with ethanol) and norcocaine
  • Sympathomimetic surge: Massive catecholamine release produces intense vasoconstriction, endothelial injury, and platelet activation throughout the microvasculature
  • Direct platelet aggregation: Cocaine promotes thrombosis in the microvasculature independent of the catecholamine surge, increasing the risk of ischemic tissue injury
  • Levamisole (adulterant): Causes immune dysregulation through T-cell abnormalities and complement activation, leading to ANCA production, neutropenia, and agranulocytosis
  • The combination produces a mixed picture with both thrombotic AND inflammatory injury patterns frequently coexisting on biopsy

Methamphetamine / Amphetamines

  • Sustained catecholamine release: Intense and prolonged release of norepinephrine and dopamine produces prolonged vasoconstriction, severe hypertension, and progressive endothelial injury
  • Direct endothelial toxicity: Methamphetamine has direct toxic effects on vascular endothelium, independent of its hemodynamic effects
  • Reported necrotizing vasculitis and polyarteritis nodosa (PAN)-like pictures have been described, though these are rare and often require exclusion of other etiologies before attribution
  • CNS vasculopathy/angiopathy: Syndromes involving cerebral vasospasm and hemorrhage have been reported; the mechanism likely involves both direct endothelial toxicity and hemodynamic injury from severe hypertension

Pharmacokinetics & Mechanism of Action

Understanding the pharmacokinetics of each stimulant is essential for predicting the time course of vascular injury, interpreting urine drug screens, and guiding management decisions.

Cocaine (Benzoylmethylecgonine)

ParameterValue
Molecular Weight303.4 g/mol
Routes of AdministrationInsufflation (nasal), smoked (crack), IV, oral (gingival)
Onset of ActionInsufflation: 1–5 min; IV/smoked: 10–60 sec; Oral: 10–30 min
Duration of Action15–30 min (smoked/IV); 45–90 min (insufflated)
Half-life~1 hour (range 0.7–1.5 hours)
MetabolismHepatic: plasma cholinesterases & hepatic esterases → benzoylecgonine (major, inactive) and ecgonine methyl ester. With ethanol → cocaethylene (active, t½ ~5h)
Detection Window (Urine)Benzoylecgonine: 2–4 days (up to 10 days with heavy use)

Mechanism of Action

  • Monoamine reuptake inhibition: Blocks the presynaptic dopamine (DAT), norepinephrine (NET), and serotonin (SERT) transporters, preventing reuptake and causing accumulation of catecholamines in the synaptic cleft
  • Sodium channel blockade: At higher doses, cocaine blocks voltage-gated sodium channels (type Ia antiarrhythmic effect), contributing to QRS prolongation, dysrhythmias, and local anesthetic toxicity
  • Peripheral vasoconstriction: Norepinephrine excess at alpha-1 receptors produces intense arteriolar vasoconstriction, reducing tissue perfusion and causing end-organ ischemia
  • Prothrombotic effects: Enhances platelet aggregation, increases plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), and promotes thromboxane A2 production, creating a hypercoagulable state

Methamphetamine

ParameterValue
Molecular Weight149.2 g/mol
Routes of AdministrationSmoked (crystal), oral, insufflation, IV, rectal
Onset of ActionSmoked/IV: immediate; Oral/insufflation: 15–30 min
Duration of Action8–24 hours (much longer than cocaine)
Half-life10–12 hours (range 6–15 hours; pH dependent)
MetabolismHepatic: CYP2D6 → amphetamine (active metabolite) + 4-hydroxymethamphetamine. Renal elimination increased in acidic urine.
Detection Window (Urine)1–4 days (up to 7 days with heavy use)

Mechanism of Action

  • Monoamine release: Unlike cocaine (a reuptake inhibitor), methamphetamine enters the presynaptic terminal via DAT/NET and reverses the transporter, causing massive efflux of dopamine and norepinephrine into the synapse
  • Vesicular disruption: Displaces monoamines from storage vesicles (via VMAT2 inhibition), flooding the cytoplasm and further increasing synaptic release
  • MAO inhibition: Weakly inhibits monoamine oxidase (MAO), reducing catecholamine degradation and prolonging the sympathomimetic effect
  • Prolonged vasoconstriction: The long half-life (10–12h vs. cocaine’s ~1h) means sustained alpha-1 mediated vasoconstriction, leading to more progressive ischemic injury over hours
  • Direct vascular toxicity: Induces oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in endothelial cells independent of hemodynamic effects, promoting endothelial apoptosis

Amphetamine (Dextroamphetamine)

Key structural difference: Amphetamine has a primary amine (–NH₂) whereas methamphetamine has a secondary amine (–NHCH₃). The N-methyl group increases lipophilicity and CNS penetration, making methamphetamine more potent and longer-lasting.

Mechanism of Action

  • Same core mechanism as methamphetamine: Reverses DAT and NET transporters, causing monoamine efflux into the synapse; also disrupts vesicular storage via VMAT2
  • Lower potency: Without the N-methyl group, amphetamine has lower lipophilicity and reduced CNS penetration, resulting in less intense euphoria and lower abuse potential at equivalent doses
  • Peripheral sympathomimetic effects: Alpha-1 and beta-1 receptor stimulation from norepinephrine excess produces hypertension, tachycardia, and vasoconstriction, though typically less severe than methamphetamine
  • Vascular injury is less common at therapeutic doses but has been reported in overdose and chronic high-dose misuse, producing vasospasm-mediated ischemia

Levamisole (Cocaine Adulterant)

ParameterValue
Molecular Weight204.3 g/mol
ContextVeterinary antihelminthic; found in >70% of North American street cocaine at peak prevalence
Half-life5–6 hours (but immunologic effects persist weeks)
MetabolismHepatic → aminorex (active metabolite with amphetamine-like properties) & other metabolites
DetectionNOT on standard urine drug screens. Requires specific GC-MS or LC-MS/MS testing. Often has already been cleared by the time of clinical presentation.

Mechanism of Action (Immunotoxicity)

  • ANCA induction: Triggers autoantibody production (both anti-MPO and anti-PR3, often with atypical dual positivity), driving autoimmune small vessel vasculitis
  • Agranulocytosis: Direct bone marrow toxicity leads to severe neutropenia (ANC <500), placing patients at high risk for secondary infections
  • Complement activation: Causes complement consumption (low C3/C4) with immune complex deposition along vessel walls
  • T-cell dysregulation: Alters T-cell function as an immunomodulator, contributing to the mixed autoimmune-thrombotic injury pattern
  • Aminorex metabolite: Levamisole is metabolized to aminorex, a known amphetamine analog, which may contribute to the euphoria-enhancing effects that drive its use as a cocaine adulterant
Clinical Pearl: The dramatically different half-lives of cocaine (~1 hour) vs. methamphetamine (10–12 hours) vs. levamisole’s immunologic effects (weeks) have direct clinical implications. Cocaine-levamisole vasculitis can present days after last use because the immunologic cascade persists well beyond drug clearance. Methamphetamine vasospasm may worsen over 12–24 hours as the drug is slowly metabolized. Always ask about timing of last use and correlate with the expected pharmacokinetic profile.

Notable Mentions: Novel Psychoactive Substances & Vascular Injury

While the classic stimulants (cocaine, methamphetamine, amphetamine) and levamisole cause true vasculitis—inflammation and destruction of the blood vessel wall—many novel psychoactive substances (NPS) cause end-organ damage and tissue necrosis through severe vasospasm and thrombotic microangiopathy rather than primary immune-mediated inflammation. This distinction is critical for ED management.

Key Clinical Distinction: True vasculitis (e.g., CLIVS) requires immunosuppression (steroids) and supportive care. Severe vasospasm (e.g., Bromo-DragonFLY toxicity) is a mechanical problem requiring aggressive, immediate vasodilation. A correct diagnosis drives an entirely different treatment pathway.

VASOSPASTIC Bromo-DragonFLY, DOX Series & NBOMe Series

These agents are notorious in toxicology for causing limb-threatening ischemia, but it is not a vasculitis.

Mechanism

Bromo-DragonFLY, the DOX series (DOM, DOB, DOI), and the NBOMe series (e.g., 25I-NBOMe) are extremely potent, high-affinity 5-HT2A receptor agonists, often with overlapping α-1 adrenergic agonism.

Clinical Picture

Profound, prolonged peripheral vasospasm closely mimicking severe ergotism. Patients present with cold, pulseless, cyanotic extremities. Without rapid vasodilation, this progresses to gangrene requiring amputation.

Angiographic Distinction: Imaging shows diffuse, smooth arterial narrowing (spasm) rather than the “beading” or inflammatory micro-thrombi characteristic of true vasculitis.

SYMPATHOMIMETIC Cathinones (“Bath Salts”) & MDMA

These agents primarily cause vascular complications secondary to massive catecholamine release.

Cathinones (MDPV, Mephedrone, Methcathinone)

Powerful norepinephrine and dopamine reuptake inhibitors/releasers. They cause intense acute hypertension, tachycardia, and vasospasm leading to myocardial infarction, stroke, or excited delirium. True necrotizing vasculitis is exceptionally rare compared to classic methamphetamine.

Tox Pearl: Intravenous use of home-cooked methcathinone (ephedrone) is famously associated with irreversible Parkinsonism due to manganese toxicity (from the potassium permanganate used in synthesis), rather than vascular pathology.

MDMA (Ecstasy)

There are isolated case reports of a Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN)-like systemic necrotizing vasculitis associated with massive MDMA use, but it is exceedingly rare. Usually, vascular collapse or microvascular issues with MDMA are secondary to hyperthermia-induced Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC).

SEROTONERGIC PMA/PMMA & The 2C-Series

Vascular pathology is not the hallmark of these drugs.

PMA & PMMA

Highly lethal substituted amphetamines acting as massive serotonin releasers and MAO inhibitors. The primary mechanism of death is severe serotonin toxicity, lethal hyperthermia, and drug-induced seizures. Any vascular damage is typically a post-mortem finding secondary to hyperthermic multi-organ failure.

2C-Series (2C-B, 2C-I, etc.)

These phenethylamines have a lower affinity for 5-HT2A compared to the NBOMe series. Primarily hallucinogenic with mild-to-moderate sympathomimetic effects. Not classically associated with severe vasospasm or vasculitis.

Clinical Presentation

📌 Cutaneous Findings (Most Common) ▼
  • Retiform purpura: Net-like, branching purpuric lesions that strongly suggest microvascular occlusion or vasculitis rather than simple thrombocytopenia. This pattern reflects the branching architecture of dermal vessels undergoing occlusion or inflammation.
  • Violaceous plaques: Deep purple-red raised lesions that may progress to bullae formation. These represent areas of deeper vascular compromise with dermal edema and hemorrhage.
  • Bullae and necrosis: Fluid-filled blisters overlying ischemic skin, progressing to black eschar if tissue perfusion is not restored. These lesions indicate full-thickness ischemic injury.
  • Tender lesions: Pain out of proportion to the visual appearance of the lesion is a key feature that distinguishes vasculitis/vasculopathy from non-tender purpura seen in thrombocytopenic states.

Distribution Clues

  • Levamisole-cocaine pattern: Ears (especially the helices), nose, cheeks, and distal extremities. This distribution is highly characteristic and should immediately raise suspicion.
  • Extremities in general, lower > upper extremity involvement
  • Lesions are often multifocal and bilateral

🏥 Systemic Involvement ▼
  • ENT: Midline destructive lesions of the nose, septum, and palate in chronic intranasal cocaine users. These lesions can mimic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA/Wegener's), but the ANCA pattern and tissue findings typically differ on careful analysis.
  • Renal: Hematuria, proteinuria, and acute kidney injury may represent immune complex glomerulonephritis or ischemic nephropathy. Urinalysis with microscopy is essential for early detection.
  • Pulmonary: Hemoptysis, dyspnea, and diffuse alveolar hemorrhage are uncommon but serious manifestations that may warrant urgent bronchoscopy for confirmation and source localization.
  • Neurologic: Headache, focal deficits, and stroke may represent vasospasm, hypertensive crisis, or rarely true CNS vasculitis. Neuroimaging is required for any focal neurologic findings.
  • Musculoskeletal: Arthralgias, arthritis, and myalgias are common systemic inflammatory accompaniments that may precede or accompany cutaneous findings.
🚨 Red Flags — Dangerous Mimics ▼

The following features should immediately raise concern for a dangerous mimic rather than stimulant vasculitis:

  • IV drug use + murmur + fever + purpura → Endocarditis with septic emboli — obtain blood cultures and echocardiography urgently
  • Rapidly spreading purpura + hemodynamic instability → Purpura fulminans / DIC / meningococcemia — aggressive resuscitation and empiric antibiotics
  • Skin changes + crepitus + pain out of proportion + fever → NSTI (surgical emergency) — immediate surgical consultation; imaging does not rule out NSTI
  • New neurologic symptoms + purpura + thrombocytopenia + renal failure → TTP — emergent plasma exchange evaluation
  • Renal disease history + painful skin calcifications + livedo → Calciphylaxis — wound care, sodium thiosulfate, nephrology consultation

Workup and Diagnosis

History (High-Yield Points)

  • Exact substance: cocaine vs. methamphetamine vs. prescription amphetamines. Determine route (intranasal, IV, smoked), frequency, last use, and total duration of use.
  • New supply: Ask specifically about a new supply, dealer, or batch — levamisole content varies by source and batch, and a change in supplier may correlate with symptom onset.
  • Constitutional symptoms: Fever, weight loss, and fatigue suggest a systemic inflammatory or infectious process that extends beyond cutaneous disease.
  • Prior episodes: Recurrence with re-exposure is a hallmark of levamisole-cocaine vasculopathy and strongly supports the diagnosis.
  • IV drug use history: Skin and soft tissue infections, prior hospitalizations, and endocarditis risk factors must be specifically elicited.
  • Autoimmune history: Oral ulcers, chronic sinus disease, hematuria, Raynaud phenomenon — to help exclude primary autoimmune vasculitis.
  • Prescription stimulants: ADHD medications (methylphenidate, dextroamphetamine) in children and adults.

Physical Exam

  • Fully characterize and document/photograph all skin lesions: location, size, borders, color, presence of bullae or necrosis, and tenderness
  • Ears specifically — the levamisole pattern characteristically involves the helices of the ears
  • Distal perfusion, pulses, and capillary refill
  • Full neurologic examination
  • Cardiac exam — murmurs raise concern for endocarditis
  • ENT: nasal septum integrity, palate examination

Core Lab Tests

  • CBC with differential — neutropenia/agranulocytosis is a key finding in levamisole-cocaine exposure
  • CMP — electrolytes, creatinine, liver function
  • ESR and CRP — nonspecific but elevation suggests active inflammation
  • Urinalysis with microscopy + urine protein/creatinine ratio — hematuria, casts, and proteinuria suggest renal vasculitis
  • CK — if ischemia or rhabdomyolysis is a concern
  • ECG and troponin — if chest pain or cocaine use (evaluate for cocaine-associated ACS)
  • Urine drug screen — supports exposure but does not detect levamisole and does not rule out exposure with a negative result

Vasculitis / Autoimmune Panel

  • ANCA (MPO and PR3) — often positive in levamisole-cocaine; may show atypical patterns (dual positivity for both MPO and PR3)
  • ANA, anti-dsDNA
  • Complement levels (C3, C4) — low in active immune complex disease
  • Cryoglobulins
  • Hepatitis B and C serologies — secondary vasculitis mimics
  • HIV — immunosuppression changes the differential
  • Blood cultures if febrile, IVDU, or embolic concern

Definitive Testing

Skin biopsy is the most important diagnostic test and should be obtained early if it will change management. A punch biopsy from the active edge of a lesion provides optimal histopathologic yield. Findings may include leukocytoclastic vasculitis (neutrophilic infiltrate with vessel wall destruction and red cell extravasation), thrombotic vasculopathy (intravascular thrombi without significant inflammation), or both patterns coexisting. Coordinate with dermatology and/or rheumatology for biopsy procurement and interpretation, including direct immunofluorescence.

💡 Diagnostic Triad: The clinical triad of retiform purpura (especially involving the ears) + ANCA positivity + neutropenia in a cocaine user is highly suggestive of levamisole-adulterated cocaine vasculopathy. Skin biopsy confirms the pattern and excludes other causes.

Management

1

Immediate Stabilization

  • ABCs: Assess airway, breathing, and hemodynamic status. Stimulant-using patients may present with concurrent sympathomimetic toxicity requiring simultaneous management.
  • Aggressive pain management: Stimulant vasculitis lesions are intensely painful. Initiate early multimodal analgesia including non-opioid and opioid agents as indicated.
  • Wound care: Apply non-occlusive dressings for open or necrotic lesions. Avoid disrupting intact bullae, which serve as a biological dressing.
  • Hypertensive crisis: If present, treat with labetalol or nicardipine infusion. Avoid pure beta-blockers with cocaine due to the risk of unopposed alpha-mediated vasospasm.
  • Establish IV access and continuous monitoring.
2

Exclude Infection First (Critical)

⚠️ DO NOT initiate steroids or immunosuppression until infection has been reasonably excluded. Immunosuppression in the setting of unrecognized endocarditis, NSTI, or sepsis is potentially lethal.
  • Endocarditis concern: Obtain blood cultures ×2 from separate sites, order TEE, and begin empiric antibiotics per institutional protocol for IVDU-associated endocarditis if clinical suspicion exists.
  • NSTI concern: Surgical consultation immediately. CT imaging may support but does not rule out NSTI; clinical concern mandates operative exploration.
  • Febrile + neutropenic (levamisole): Treat as febrile neutropenia with broad-spectrum antibiotics, reverse isolation precautions, and hematology consultation.
3

Stimulant-Specific Management

  • Drug cessation is the single most important intervention. Recurrence with re-exposure is well-documented and predictable. Counsel the patient directly about the connection between their drug use and their illness, and connect them with addiction medicine and/or substance use disorder services.
  • Supportive wound care: For limited cutaneous disease, engage the wound care team. Topical analgesia and plastics/burn team consultation are appropriate for extensive or deep necrosis requiring debridement.
  • Corticosteroids: Considered for significant inflammatory vasculitis after infection has been excluded. Evidence is limited and largely case-series based; benefit is variable, and agent/dose has not been standardized. This decision should involve toxicology, rheumatology, and/or dermatology.
  • Neutropenia/agranulocytosis: Implement infection precautions. Treat febrile neutropenia per institutional protocol. Consider G-CSF (filgrastim) in severe or prolonged cases. Consult hematology if counts do not recover.
4

Consultations

  • Toxicology — primary co-management, drug-specific guidance, and cessation counseling
  • Dermatology — biopsy procurement, lesion characterization, and histopathologic interpretation
  • Rheumatology — immunology workup, ANCA interpretation, and steroid/immunosuppression decisions
  • Nephrology — if hematuria, proteinuria, or rising creatinine suggests renal involvement
  • Neurology — if CNS symptoms or focal neurologic findings are present
  • Hematology — if significant or refractory neutropenia/agranulocytosis
  • Plastics / Burns / Wound Team — for extensive necrosis requiring debridement or reconstruction

Monitoring and Disposition

🚨 Admit If Any of the Following

  • Systemic organ involvement — renal failure, pulmonary hemorrhage, or neurologic symptoms
  • Rapidly progressive skin necrosis or large areas of cutaneous involvement
  • Uncontrolled pain despite oral analgesia
  • Fever in the setting of neutropenia (levamisole-related agranulocytosis) — treat urgently as febrile neutropenia
  • Clinical concern for endocarditis, NSTI, or septic emboli — admit for definitive workup
  • Significant lab derangements: creatinine elevation, cytopenias, or coagulopathy

✅ Outpatient Management Only If ALL of the Following

  • Limited skin findings only, stable or slowly evolving
  • Hemodynamically stable and afebrile
  • Reassuring lab results — normal renal function, no significant neutropenia
  • Reliable follow-up confirmed with dermatology and/or rheumatology within 48–72 hours
  • Clear written return precautions provided and understood by the patient
  • Patient able to care for wounds at home with appropriate supplies

Return Precautions (Provide to ALL Patients at Discharge)

Instruct the patient to return to the emergency department immediately for any of the following:

  • Spreading redness, warmth, or new skin breakdown
  • Fever or chills
  • Shortness of breath or coughing up blood
  • Blood in urine
  • New weakness, numbness, difficulty speaking or walking
  • Worsening pain despite prescribed medications

Documentation Best Practices

  • Map all lesion borders at the time of initial evaluation using anatomic diagrams or clinical photography
  • Photograph lesions if institutional policy permits, with a ruler for scale
  • Document last drug use, substance type, route of administration, and any recent change in supply

Clinical Cases

Case 1 — Classic Levamisole-Cocaine Pattern

Adapted from: Gulati S, Donato AA. Levamisole-induced vasculitis. N Engl J Med. 2012;367(11):e16. doi:10.1056/NEJMicm1110582; and Gross RL, Brucker J, Bahce-Altuntas A, et al. A novel cutaneous vasculitis syndrome induced by levamisole-contaminated cocaine. Clin Rheumatol. 2011;30(10):1385–1392.

Patient

A 44-year-old man with a history of regular cocaine use presents with 5 days of progressive painful skin lesions. He reports using cocaine from a "new batch" approximately one week prior to the onset of symptoms.

Presentation

On examination, there are tender retiform purpuric lesions involving bilateral ear helices, the forehead, and bilateral lower extremities. The lesions have central pallor with a livedo-like violaceous border. The patient reports significant pain and diffuse arthralgia for the past 3 days. He is afebrile with stable vital signs. He denies IV drug use.

Workup
  • CBC: WBC 1.8 (ANC 0.6 — agranulocytosis range), Hgb 12.4, Plt 198
  • CMP: Creatinine 1.3 (baseline unknown), all others normal
  • UA: 2+ blood, 1+ protein
  • ANCA: Positive (atypical pattern — both MPO and PR3 weakly positive)
  • ANA: Negative; Complement: mildly low C3
  • Blood cultures: No growth
  • UDS: Positive for cocaine metabolites (levamisole not detected by standard assay)
Skin Biopsy

Punch biopsy from the active edge of a lower extremity lesion showed leukocytoclastic vasculitis with perivascular neutrophilic infiltrate and red cell extravasation. A thrombotic component was seen in some dermal vessels. Direct immunofluorescence demonstrated IgM and C3 deposition along vessel walls.

Hospital Course

The patient was admitted for neutropenia management and wound care. Infectious workup returned negative. Rheumatology and dermatology co-managed the case. Cessation counseling was initiated with warm handoff to the substance use disorder team. Supportive care with wound care team was provided. Steroids were deferred initially given a mild inflammatory picture and concern for infection risk with agranulocytosis. Neutrophil count recovered over 7 days. Skin lesions stabilized and began to improve.

Outcome

Discharged with close rheumatology and dermatology follow-up. Unfortunately, the patient had complete recurrence of lesions 3 weeks later after re-using cocaine — reinforcing the critical importance of drug cessation as the primary therapeutic intervention.

Case 2 — Methamphetamine Vascular Injury Mimic

Adapted from: Lappin JM, Darke S, Farrell M. Methamphetamine use and future risk for Parkinson’s disease: Evidence and clinical implications. Drug Alcohol Depend. 2018;187:134–140; and Westover AN, McBride S, Haley RW. Stroke in young adults who abuse amphetamines or cocaine: a population-based study of hospitalized patients. Arch Gen Psychiatry. 2007;64(4):495–502.

Patient

A 32-year-old man with heavy methamphetamine use presents via EMS after being found unresponsive by bystanders. The scene suggested prolonged down-time.

Presentation

On arrival: BP 210/130 mmHg, HR 115. Bystanders reported the patient complained of severe headache before becoming unresponsive. On examination, the patient is unresponsive to voice but localizes to pain. A right-sided facial droop and right arm weakness are noted. There is mottled skin of bilateral lower extremities.

Workup
  • CT head (non-contrast): Hypertensive hemorrhage — left basal ganglia intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) with surrounding edema and early midline shift
  • Labs: CBC normal, Creatinine 1.1, CK 2,200 (rhabdomyolysis from prolonged down-time)
  • UA: 3+ blood (myoglobinuria vs. hematuria)
  • UDS: Positive for methamphetamine and amphetamine
  • ANCA, ANA, ACLA: All negative
  • ECG: Sinus tachycardia, no ischemic changes
Acute Management
  • Neurosurgery emergently consulted — ICH managed conservatively (no surgical indication at initial presentation)
  • IV labetalol infusion for BP control (target SBP 140–160 mmHg)
  • Aggressive IV crystalloid for rhabdomyolysis and renal protection
  • IV mannitol for elevated intracranial pressure management
  • Reversal agents and thromboelastography checked (patient had no known anticoagulation)
Key Teaching Point
The skin mottling in this case represented diffuse vasospasm from the sympathomimetic surge, not true inflammatory vasculitis. The primary driver of hemorrhage was severe hypertension in the context of catecholamine excess. A vasculitis workup was initiated but was unrevealing. Treat the hypertension and hemodynamic emergency first — a vasculitis workup can follow if the clinical course is atypical or if deficits persist beyond the expected recovery trajectory.
Outcome

Transferred to neurocritical care. Blood pressure was gradually controlled. Rhabdomyolysis resolved with fluid therapy. Persistent right hemiplegia at hospital day 10 — the patient was transferred to an acute rehabilitation facility.

Key Pearls

🔬

"Vasculitis" vs. Vasculopathy — Know the Difference

Many cases labeled as "stimulant vasculitis" are actually thrombotic vasculopathy with limited or no true inflammatory infiltrate. On biopsy, you may see thrombi without the classic leukocytoclastic pattern of neutrophilic vessel wall destruction. Both patterns can coexist in the same patient. The distinction matters for treatment — thrombotic vasculopathy does not benefit from immunosuppression the way inflammatory vasculitis might. Only a tissue biopsy can reliably make this distinction.

👂

Ears + ANCA + Neutropenia = Levamisole Until Proven Otherwise

The triad of retiform purpura involving the ears, ANCA positivity (often with atypical dual MPO/PR3 positivity), and neutropenia in a cocaine user is pathognomonic enough to act on clinically. Levamisole is not detected on standard urine drug screens. A negative UDS does not rule out levamisole exposure. Recurrence with re-use is the rule, not the exception — making this triad a powerful diagnostic indicator.

🚫

No Steroids Until Infection Is Excluded — Full Stop

Initiating steroids in a patient with unrecognized NSTI, endocarditis with septic emboli, or sepsis is potentially lethal. The workup must include blood cultures, echocardiography in appropriate risk patients, and surgical consultation if NSTI is in the differential, before immunosuppression is considered. When in doubt about infection — call surgery first, not rheumatology.

💊

Cessation Is the Most Effective Treatment

The most powerful intervention in stimulant vasculitis is stopping the causative drug. Recurrence is predictably associated with re-use, and no amount of wound care, steroids, or immunosuppression will prevent further vascular injury if the patient continues using. Every encounter is an opportunity for a brief motivational intervention and warm handoff to addiction medicine or substance use disorder services.

✂️

Biopsy Early If It Will Change Management

Skin biopsy from the active edge of a lesion is the single most informative diagnostic test. Waiting until the patient is "more stable" often means the active lesion has evolved and biopsy yield decreases as inflammation resolves or necrosis replaces viable tissue. If you are considering immunosuppression, you need a tissue diagnosis first. Coordinate early with dermatology to punch biopsy a fresh, active lesion before treatment alters the histopathology.
